The Bridge of the Three Little Ducklings

This story aims to strengthen the discipline needed to take on any task, while cultivating unity, trust in family, and freedom, even in the middle of bold actions or possible mistakes. Animal psychology: cooperative, free, friendly, and supportive. It's said that when a flock of ducks or geese takes flight and one falls to the ground, two others come down to keep it company until another flock passes and they join it, taking to the air again together.

Once upon a time…… there were three grumbling little ducklings who went every morning to a river with their mother duck, to wash and clean their little faces in the water.

Between yawns and complaints they swam, cheering each other on, but their mother firmly forbade them from crossing to the other bank, warning them how difficult and dangerous the river's current could be. Every morning, out of habit, the little birds walked to the river, but as they grew and the vegetation grew thicker, they could no longer enjoy swimming in the small patch they had chosen; disappointed, they would wash their faces and splash just a little water.

Time passed, and the ducklings walked there every day between complaints, staring at the water, the plants, the rocks, everything….without ever being able to swim across or finally cross the river.

They looked at each other, unsettled, not knowing what to do, unable to understand why Mother Nature had closed off the stretch of river they used each morning to wash their little faces. But out of caution and respect for what Mother Duck had told them, they never crossed, always confused, because on the other side the water looked clear, with little vegetation, perfect for swimming.

The ducklings grew up, and could now go to the river on their own.

One day…… a wonderful idea came to them. ...To build a bridge over the water, from one edge of the land to the other, so they could finally know more of the whole mysterious river.

They worked hard, first thinking through how they'd build the bridge, gathering materials, calculating how many trees they'd need for the wood, even how to use the plants growing inside the water to decorate and make the bridge more beautiful.

They worked until they were exhausted. The three duck brothers strove to build a bridge that was not only strong and big, but also safe and beautiful — and in record time.

The day they finished…….. the oldest brother proposed to the others that they swim to the other side of the river.

The middle brother refused. He didn't see the point of swimming when — thank goodness — the bridge was already finished. The youngest brother loved the idea. Besides testing the bridge, he could also disobey their mother and prove that despite how dangerous the river's current was, nature could be defied. Both agreed: —Yes, I'm in — we already built a bridge, but it'll be used only by the cowards who don't want to swim! Splash….!

The oldest and the youngest, in the blink of an eye, began swimming at great speed, pushing aside plants and stones through the river's dense water, and within half an hour: "they made it across."

As easy as it would have been to cross using the bridge they'd just built, they achieved it instead through a wild contest of defying nature.

Meanwhile…the other duckling was left frightened, crying, frozen, watching the daring feat his two little brothers were pulling off.

Their mother went down to the river to see what was happening with her children, who by habit came home early.

When she arrived — what did she see?.... the middle son crying over his two little brothers. Looking further off, she saw her two other sons, soaking wet, shaking their little tails and swinging their heads back and forth in triumph.

—What a fright! she said, desperate. —Son, explain to me why they're wet, if you were all building the bridge. She couldn't believe they had gone swimming, and went on, angry: —Oh, my God, what have you done? Why did you go swimming? I explained to you how dangerous it could be!

The duckling who hadn't crossed, listening closely, confessed sadly: —Mama, I didn't cross — not out of obedience or respect for what you've always taught us, I was simply very afraid, and that makes me a coward!

He lowered his gaze and went on……: —I don't believe in you anymore, Mama! You've always deceived me — all our lives you told us it was impossible to defy that silly river, and now here I am crying while my two little brothers jump for joy and excitement.

Their mother calmed down, understanding that this son badly needed her attention. She hugged him tightly, whispering in his ear: —Don't be discouraged, my son, you can cross too.

—How? he asked, needing an answer. —You can, my son, "through the river."

Then, looking together at the finished bridge, they decided to cross so they could share in the joy of their two wild brothers.

When they were all reunited, Mother Duck explained to everyone: —When I was very young, I ran away from my parents and wanted to do the same thing, but I got nowhere — I nearly drowned, one of my wings was hurt, and I watched many other little ducklings die, victims of a powerful current. Recalling this, her voice a little faint, she said: —I love you so, so much, and I couldn't bear it if something hurt you; forgive me for telling you it was impossible, difficult, or dangerous, but I only warned you to spare you a terrible experience like the one I lived through. Please, don't try it again!

She kissed all three sons, now reunited, and squeezing one of the middle son's little wings — he was still drying a tear — she said: —They've swum across and are very happy about it. You're grown now, you're free, and it's your choice — do you want to try?

The duckling smiled and shook his head, a clear "no": —Better not, Mama. I'll always cross — but only over the bridge.

And they say that ever since, all the duck families who go to the river to wash and clean their little faces in the mornings, though far from cowardly, only cross the river by the bridge already built, and when they grow up they'd rather fly to the other side of the river than swim, as those daring, wild brothers once did.
